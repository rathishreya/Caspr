Go-to-market · Position and funnel · Internal

We do not describe the work. We publish it.

Caspr is sold on one standard: every source credible, every alternative weighed, every conclusion defensible. The marketing is built to the same standard. What follows is the whole chain — the position, the buyer, the channels, what we publish and why, and the twelve weeks that carry a stranger to a paying account.

The position: not an assistant, an analyst.

Caspr is Analytical AI — purpose-built for business analysis, not conversation. The promise is Arrive certain. Three rules follow, and they are absolute: never name a competitor in lead copy; never frame proof as work for the reader; cost is the third act, never the hook.

The purpose is revenue, not attention.

Carry a defined buyer from first contact to paid, repeatably. Not awareness, not reach. One number decides whether we scale: x — revenue earned per $1 of total spend, by signup cohort, read at three months and six. Below 2 it does not pay for itself; above it we scale while it holds.

A funnel measured from signup would report an activation rate that means nothing. The alias at step eight is the single most important call in the tracking spec: without it, everything a visitor did before signing up is orphaned.

The 1,600 people already signed up are a bonus, not the plan.

That cohort arrived through positioning we have retired, pricing we have replaced and a website that contradicted the product. Fewer than two of them ever paid — but that number describes the acquisition, not the product, and using it to forecast anything would be forecasting the wrong company. We re-engage them in week five, once the new funnel has shown us where it leaks, and we report them as a separate series so they can never flatter the number that matters.

Roughly 150 signups a month. 40% reach a first analysis. 3–5% convert to paid on today's assumption — and the bet of this whole plan is that the right buyer, correctly addressed, takes that to 10%. Doubling conversion is worth more than doubling reach, and costs far less.

Four things get made. The research decides which.

Every choice on this page was taken from the buyer research rather than invented over it — eight day-in-the-life profiles, with the pain and the objections in the buyers' own words.

Six of eight buyers describe the same scene.

Not the same pain — the same moment. Someone senior asks where the number came from. A partner, a board, a general partner, an investor, an interviewer. Six different rooms, one question. That is the spine of everything we publish.

The biggest objection has an answer nobody has written.

Is this allowed at my firm? appears in every one of the six objection tables — as AI policy, procurement, compliance review, agency IT, client confidentiality, a dissertation supervisor. Nothing on the site addresses it. Ten evergreen pieces do.

Only two and a half items a week are written from scratch. That is what makes the volume affordable: reviewing a derivative of already-approved material takes a minute or two; reviewing origination takes ten to fifteen. Twenty-three items a week costs 62–77 minutes against 450 available.

The pillars, and the weight each carries

Five pillars. The share is a decision, not an outcome.

  • 42% · AnalystAn analyst, not an assistant. The identity, and the spine of the twelve weeks
  • 18% · CategoryAnalytical AI. Category education only — comparison pages, social, founder writing. Never a headline or an ad
  • 20% · CostA fraction of the cost. Two weeks of twelve, and the only place it leads
  • 12% · TrustYour data is your data. Raised from 5% — it answers our primary buyer's leading objection, and had no home in the plan until we counted
  • 8% · SpeedWeeks to minutes. Deliberately demoted — speed commoditises and cannot carry the position

The trust correction is the kind of thing that only surfaces when the calendar is laid out and the shares are added up. On principle it looked balanced; on arithmetic it was not.

Cited, or it does not ship. Every item carries a finding traceable to a real Caspr analysis, or an attributed opinion from a named person. An item with neither is not generated — for a company whose position is that it is not the machine producing confident unsourced text, anything less would be self-defeating.

Cost occupies two weeks, not four. Four cost-led weeks would land exactly as the first reading of x arrives — and a soft number meeting a month of price-led content invites the wrong instinct.

We attack the format, never the firms.

The incumbent research model is slow, expensive, generic, and dated the day it publishes — and all four are structural rather than fixable. But we cite those same publishers as our sources, so attacking their credibility would attack our own. The line we can defend instead: that report is excellent, it cost $12,000, it was published fourteen months ago, and it does not answer your question. Unarguable — and it leaves every source intact to cite.

What makes an issue an event rather than a blog post

Four formats. Each one is arguable by construction.

  • Correct itTake a number everybody cites and show what it rests on. That the sources disagree threefold; that the canonical figure traces to a press release. The flagship
  • The roomA full sector analysis, free, on a question a board or an investment committee is asking this quarter
  • TheirsThe audience picks the question. Readers become participants, and the vote is itself a demand signal
  • WrongWe publish our own corrections. Rare, and disproportionately powerful for a company selling defensibility

One rule across all four: every issue takes a position. A summary gets read and forgotten; a conclusion gets argued with — and the argument is the engagement we are actually after.

Topics are not chosen in a planning meeting. A four-source demand pipeline — what buyers prompt Caspr for, search trend, the questions recurring in their communities, and sector event flow — ranks the candidates with the evidence attached. A person picks from the top of that list. Not an assumption, and not an automaton.

Go-to-market · The launch · Internal

The launch is a publication, not an announcement.

The Record — an analyst house that publishes, rather than a product that shipped. Issue one is Caspr run on the market research industry itself: the instrument turned on the incumbents without a word said against them, demonstrated on the one subject this audience knows well enough to judge.

Build-up, peak, and fourteen weeks that do not flatten.

Issues run before the announcement, so launch day arrives with a body of work rather than a promise. The peak launches a recurring feature, not a one-off — a single issue gives peak attention something to read; a standing monthly index gives it a reason to subscribe. Most launches die in the weeks after; a rhythm is what survives them.

Sustained does not mean constant.

Spreading a campaign evenly across fourteen weeks turns it into a drip, and a drip is indistinguishable from business as usual. It means repeated peaks — and three of the four mechanics that produce them are work already in the plan, not work added to it.

How an issue actually travels

Sharing it has to make the sharer look good.

  • The atomA hundred pages do not move. One chart with a source line does. Every issue produces one, deliberately — legible in two seconds, standing alone
  • ProvenanceOur citation line is the distribution mechanism. A chart pasted into someone else's deck arrives with its source printed on it. No competitor's chart tells the third reader where it came from
  • Per sectorA general index is interesting; "here is yours" is shareable. Each sector's practitioners share their own row
  • CreditIf a reader adds what we could not see, we name them. A better source, local knowledge no publication holds, an alternative we missed — most of which needs us to be right, not wrong. Professional credit costs nothing and is worth more than money here
  • Same dayWhen something material happens, the full cited analysis publishes while the news is live. Nobody else can

Ruled out: asking to be shared, engagement bait, coordinated amplification, and gating anything — a gate stops a forward dead, and the forward is the whole mechanism.

The honest ceiling.

B2B research does not go viral, and planning as though it might is the mistake. A good issue is a few hundred invisible forwards inside firms, twenty to fifty substantive comments, two or three trade pickups, and one or two people citing it. That compounds — planning for a spike produces tactics that spend the brand for one week of attention.

The asset that survives a weak result.

People who will not open an account will subscribe to research. The publication builds an email list that is not a signup list — a larger, earlier audience, and the one thing here that keeps compounding even if the numbers disappoint.

Four gates — each one a number, agreed before it is needed

Nothing opens early, and nothing opens on an argument.

  • LaunchWebsite and app both live. A first impression cannot be re-spent
  • ProofTestimonials published, three real reports on the site. Gates every dollar of paid
  • FunnelWeek four: activation trending to 40%, attribution resolving, 300+ signups. Gates the campaign spend — roughly $8k, one-time
  • Scalex above 2 at six months. Gates every increase in budget. Nothing else does

Agreeing each number in advance is the point. It turns a judgement call made under pressure into a reading — and it means a launch we can decline to fire, which is the only kind worth preparing.
