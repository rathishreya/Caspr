## Executive Summary

The State of Remote Work in 2025–2026 suggests a stabilization of remote work trends rather than a reversal, with significant portions of the workforce engaging in home-based work. According to data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, about 35% of employed individuals worked remotely during 2025, corroborated by Stanford/NBER surveys estimating that 25% of U.S. workdays were remote. Globally, college-educated employees averaged one remote workday per week from 2023 to early 2025. Despite only 12% of executives planning return-to-office (RTO) mandates, these mandates are projected to slightly reduce remote workdays. This indicates a persistence in remote work habits, sustained by workers' preferences. 

Productivity Evidence reveals a positive, albeit nuanced impact of remote and hybrid work on productivity. A 2026 meta-analysis links remote work usage and its intensity to varying performance impacts, which depend on whether arrangements were voluntary, who conducted performance evaluations, and the timing of studies. At the industry level, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported associations between remote work and growth in total factor productivity (TFP), with firms seeing slower growth in unit costs. On the firm level, French studies suggested productivity gains linked to increased telework. However, hybrid work, while maintaining performance, does not necessarily enhance it. These findings signify a complex interaction between work environment and productivity.

Return-to-Office Mandates and Their Effects show limited success in decreasing remote workdays. Stanford’s 2025 survey indicates a minimal drop in home workdays, but one day per month physically in an office can increase work productivity and coworker communication, as indicated by a 2026 NBER experiment. Nonetheless, these mandates result in potential losses of experienced workers, as seen in companies like Microsoft, SpaceX, and Apple, suggesting RTO mandates can negatively impact innovation.

Well-Being, Autonomy, and Performance emphasize the importance of aligning work arrangements with employee preferences for increased retention and engagement. Misalignment can trigger disenchantment and turnover, as rigid mandates counteract worker autonomy and job satisfaction. Studies in 2025 and 2026 demonstrate that well-being impacts due to RTO mandates are marginal, yet the alignment of work conditions remains crucial.

Conclusions and Research Implications express a holistic view of remote and hybrid work, recognizing them as enduring structures with context-dependent productivity outcomes. The alignment of tasks, voluntariness, and preferences are key factors in productivity. Research should refocus to acknowledge varied productivity dynamics at the individual, team, and organizational levels. Additionally, RTO should be viewed as an intervention impacting organizational culture and human capital, not just policy about work locations. These insights suggest that understanding remote work requires a nuanced and multi-faceted approach.

## 1. The State of Remote Work in 2025–2026

Remote work appears to have stabilized rather than reversed in 2025–2026. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data show that in 2025, 35% of employed people did some or all work at home on days worked [1](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.htm), while Stanford/NBER survey-based estimates place work-from-home at about one quarter of all paid U.S. workdays in 2025 [2](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/03/return-to-office-not-everybody-is-doing-it) [3](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33951). Cross-national evidence likewise suggests persistence: average work-from-home rates among college-educated employees were roughly one day per week globally from 2023 through early 2025 [4](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/global-persistence-work-home). Stanford’s February 2025 business survey also found that only 12% of executives with hybrid or remote staff planned an RTO mandate in the next year, and even those mandates barely changed expected paid workdays at home, from 21.2% to 20.8% [5](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/policy-brief/us-executives-predict-work-home-here-stay).

## 2. Productivity Evidence: Key Findings

The 2025–2026 evidence base is mixed but leans modestly positive for remote or hybrid work when tasks are suitable and arrangements are well matched. A 2026 meta-analysis covering 89 quantitative studies reports that remote-work usage and intensity are linked to performance, with effects varying by voluntariness, rating source, and whether studies were conducted before, during, or after the pandemic [6](https://journals.aom.org/doi/full/10.5465/AMPROC.2026.40bp). At the industry level, BLS reports a positive relationship between remote work and total factor productivity across U.S. private industries, and its 2019–2023 analysis also links larger remote-work increases to lower growth in unit capital, energy, materials, and services costs [7](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/articles-and-research/remote-work/productivity-and-remote-work.htm) [8](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-13/remote-work-productivity.htm). Firm-level causal evidence remains heterogeneous: a 2025 IZA study of more than 6,500 French firms found that a 10-percentage-point increase in telework raised productivity growth by about 0.7–1.0 points in OLS and about 2.7 points in IV estimates [9](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18655/post-covid-telework-and-productivity-a-large-scale-analysis), while earlier landmark experiments found hybrid work often preserves measured performance rather than raising it outright [10](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/is-hybrid-work-the-best-of-both-worlds-evidence-from-a-field-experiment/).

## 3. Return-to-Office Mandates and Their Effects

Recent evidence suggests that RTO mandates often have limited aggregate effects on work-from-home prevalence but can impose meaningful organizational costs. Stanford’s 2025 executive survey found that announced RTOs would reduce paid workdays at home only marginally, from 21.2% to 20.8% [5](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/policy-brief/us-executives-predict-work-home-here-stay). Experimental and quasi-experimental studies indicate some targeted benefits from in-person contact: a 2026 NBER field experiment found that one office day per month increased calls handled per hour by 7.8% and added 36 minutes of coworker communication in the following week [11](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35331), while a Fortune 500 software-engineer study found proximity improves training and productivity [12](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31880). At the same time, new causal evidence presented at the 2026 AEA meetings reports significant outflows of senior employees after RTO mandates at Microsoft, SpaceX, and Apple, implying potential human-capital and innovation costs [13](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/paper/6tSeyA6E).

## 4. Well-Being, Autonomy, and Performance

The strongest consistent moderator in the 2025–2026 literature is not remote work per se, but whether the arrangement fits worker preferences, autonomy needs, and job design. Pew reported in January 2025 that many remote workers would be likely to leave their job if they could no longer work from home, underscoring preference alignment as a retention variable rather than a peripheral attitude measure [14](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/13/many-remote-workers-say-theyd-be-likely-to-leave-their-job-if-they-could-no-longer-work-from-home/). A 2025 Organizational Dynamics review argues that remote work performs better when employees have fit and autonomy, whereas rigid mandates that ignore preferences can worsen disengagement and turnover intentions [15](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S009026162500052X). On well-being specifically, a natural-experiment IZA study on an RTO mandate found little overall effect on health and well-being outcomes, with most estimates statistically insignificant and small in magnitude [16](https://docs.iza.org/dp17355.pdf).

## 5. Conclusions and Research Implications

By 2025–2026, the evidence no longer supports a simple “remote work helps” or “remote work hurts” claim. The brief consensus is that hybrid and remote arrangements are now structurally persistent, average productivity effects are modest and context-dependent, and the main moderators are task suitability, intensity, voluntariness, and preference alignment [4](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/global-persistence-work-home) [6](https://journals.aom.org/doi/full/10.5465/AMPROC.2026.40bp) [9](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18655/post-covid-telework-and-productivity-a-large-scale-analysis). For research-oriented readers, the main implication is to prioritize designs that separate individual performance, team learning, and firm-level TFP, because these margins can move in different directions at the same time [7](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/articles-and-research/remote-work/productivity-and-remote-work.htm) [11](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35331) [12](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31880). A second implication is that RTO should be studied as an organizational intervention with selection, retention, and human-capital effects, not merely as a location policy [13](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/paper/6tSeyA6E) [14](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/13/many-remote-workers-say-theyd-be-likely-to-leave-their-job-if-they-could-no-longer-work-from-home/).
