## Executive Summary

The executive summary observes the global landscape, historical shifts, and future trends in copper production and demand. As of 2025, global mine supply is projected to be approximately 24.8 million tonnes (Mt), predominantly led by Chile, which remains the top producer, followed by the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Peru, and China. The DRC emerges as a significant player, rapidly growing as a major supplier and overtaking Peru as the second-largest producer. Concurrently, historical shifts since 2000 have seen the DRC transform from a minor player to a copper powerhouse, backed by substantial production increases in Peru and China. Despite these dynamics, Chile’s production has remained relatively steady due to challenges like declining ore grades and aging mines.

Copper’s demand is poised for significant growth, driven primarily by the transition to electric vehicles, renewable energy, grid expansion, and advancements in digital infrastructure. Forecasts from the International Energy Agency suggest copper will experience the largest growth among energy minerals, with demand projected to increase by about 7 Mt by 2040. S&P Global anticipates copper demand will rise from approximately 28.4 Mt in 2025 to 42 Mt by 2040, with a significant contribution from the energy transition sector, which alone will grow from 8.5 Mt in 2025 to 15.6 Mt in 2040. BHP highlights the transportation sector's growing share of copper demand as electric vehicle (EV) adoption expands.

Supply constraints and price outlook are highlighted as critical challenges for the copper industry. An impending supply shortfall of 14 million tonnes by 2040 necessitates significant supply augmentation to match demand. Price pressures are expected to be intense due to challenges like ore grade declines, rising capital costs, and complex project developments. These factors combine with slow mine development timelines due to permitting complexities and environmental concerns, contributing to a structurally tight market outlook through 2040.

Geopolitical risks and strategic implications have also evolved. The focus has expanded from mining to control over processing and export policies, with China leading in the global development of copper-smelting capacity since 2005 and projected to hold a majority share by 2025. Policies in other nations, such as the DRC's export ban on copper and cobalt concentrates in 2026, further illustrate the geopolitical complexities. For businesses, maintaining supply security entails diversification of mine origins, smelting/refining locations, and trade-policy exposure, beyond merely securing copper ore access. These multifaceted challenges underscore the strategic importance of adapting to the evolving geopolitical and market landscape.

## 1. Global Production Landscape

Global mine supply remains highly concentrated: USGS estimates 2025 output at about 24.8 Mt, led by Chile at 5.0 Mt, the DRC at 3.2 Mt, Peru at 2.6 Mt and China at 1.8 Mt, with the USA and Indonesia both near 1.1 Mt and Russia, Australia, Kazakhstan and Mexico rounding out the top ten [1](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026.pdf) [2](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/ditcinf2025d2.pdf). For 2024, the same ranking broadly held, with Chile at 5.3 Mt, DRC 3.3 Mt, Peru 2.6 Mt, China 1.8 Mt, USA 1.1 Mt, Indonesia 1.1 Mt, Russia 0.93 Mt, Australia 0.80 Mt, Kazakhstan 0.74 Mt and Mexico 0.70 Mt [1](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026.pdf) [3](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/minerals-mining/mining-data-statistics-analysis/minerals-metals-facts/copper-facts?wbdisable=false). Chile still dominates on reserves and scale, but the DRC has become the clear No. 2 producer and the fastest-rising major supplier, while Peru remains the third pillar of mined copper supply [1](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026.pdf) [2](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/ditcinf2025d2.pdf).

## 2. Historical Shifts in Production

Since 2000, the biggest structural change has been the rise of the DRC from a secondary producer into a top-tier copper power, while Peru expanded strongly through large mine builds and China steadily increased domestic mine output and, even more importantly, downstream processing capacity [2](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/ditcinf2025d2.pdf) [4](https://www.fastmarkets.com/insights/how-has-the-drc-become-a-powerhouse-in-the-copper-industry/) [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary). Chile remains the largest producer, but its mined output has largely plateaued around the 5.0-5.7 Mt range over the last decade as ore grades declined, mines aged and project complexity increased [1](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026.pdf) [6](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures/). UNCTAD notes that from 2000 to 2024 the DRC’s share of global copper mine output rose from about 1% to 14%, Peru’s from 7% to 11%, and China’s from 5% to 8%, while Chile’s production fell about 5% over the same long period despite retaining first place [2](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/ditcinf2025d2.pdf).

## 3. Energy Transition and Demand Drivers

Copper demand is being pulled simultaneously by EVs, renewable power, grid expansion and digital infrastructure, with the IEA saying copper records the largest absolute volume growth among key energy minerals, adding about 7 Mt by 2040, and with demand in its Net Zero pathway rising roughly 50% by 2040 [7](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/outlook) [8](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2024/outlook-for-key-minerals?_hsmi=358901176). S&P Global projects total copper demand rising from about 28.4 Mt in 2025 to roughly 42 Mt in 2040, driven by four vectors: core economic demand, the energy transition, AI/data centers and defense, with energy-transition demand alone rising from 8.5 Mt in 2025 to 15.6 Mt in 2040 [9](https://www.spglobal.com/en/research-insights/special-reports/look-forward/energy-futures/copper-in-fractured-world) [10](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2026/01/the-copper-conundrum-why-meeting-ai-era-electrification-demands-is-a-race-against-time). BHP similarly expects transport’s share of copper demand to rise from around 11% in 2021 to over 20% by 2040 as EV adoption scales, while data-center copper demand could increase six-fold by 2050 as AI lifts electricity use [11](https://www.bhp.com/-/media/project/bhp1ip/bhp-com-en/documents/news/2024/240930_bhpinsights_howcopperwillshapeourfuture.pdf).

## 4. Supply Constraints and Price Outlook

The market outlook remains structurally tight: S&P Global estimates a 10 Mt copper supply shortfall by 2040, while its broader framing says an additional 14 Mt of supply will be needed by 2040 to satisfy demand growth [12](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/metals/010826-copper-supply-gap-to-widen-24-by-2040-as-electrification-accelerates-study) [10](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2026/01/the-copper-conundrum-why-meeting-ai-era-electrification-demands-is-a-race-against-time). The IEA reported record copper prices in 2026 alongside severe smelter margin pressure, with TC/RCs collapsing as concentrate supply tightened; it also highlights declining ore grades, capital inflation and rising project complexity as core barriers to bringing on new mines fast enough [6](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures/) [13](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures). In practical terms, the industry faces a double squeeze through 2035-2040: demand is accelerating faster than mine development timelines, while permitting, water, power, social license and lower-grade deposits are making new greenfield supply slower and more expensive [6](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures/) [12](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/metals/010826-copper-supply-gap-to-widen-24-by-2040-as-electrification-accelerates-study).

## 5. Geopolitical Risks and Strategic Implications

Copper supply-chain risk is no longer just about mines; it is increasingly about processing control and export policy. The IEA says China accounted for more than 90% of global copper-smelting capacity growth since 2005 and reached about 50% of global smelting output by 2025, while its share of refined copper production was about 45% in 2024 and is set to keep rising on the current pipeline [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary) [14](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/ef5e9b70-3374-4caa-ba9d-19c72253bfc4/GlobalCriticalMineralsOutlook2025.pdf). Policy risk is also broadening across producing states: the DRC moved in August 2026 to ban exports of copper and cobalt concentrates, while USGS documents recent export controls or bans by China, Zimbabwe and Mozambique across critical minerals, refined metals or scrap streams that affect wider metals security and processing economics [15](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/congo-bans-exports-of-copper-cobalt-concentrates-official-order-says-ce7f50ddd988ff21) [1](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026.pdf). For business, the implication is clear: supply security now depends on diversification across mine origin, smelting/refining geography and trade-policy exposure, not simply access to copper units in the ground [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary) [6](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/copper-prices-have-hit-record-highs-but-smelters-face-mounting-strategic-pressures/).
